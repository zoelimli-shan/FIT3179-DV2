Australia's Conservation Choices

1. Monitored populations fell to 42% of their 2000 level

Australia’s national score for monitored threatened populations starts at 1 in 2000. It reached its low of 0.383 in 2019 and stood at 0.417 in 2022. Reptiles had the lowest 2022 group score, while mammals declined less steeply. The figures make the scale of the challenge clear before we turn to the choices made in response.

The shared decline hides sharply different paths between groups.

2. Three in four newer priorities were absent from the earlier list

The 2015–2020 Threatened Species Strategy set 71 priorities for recovery. The 2022–2032 Threatened Species Action Plan names 110, including some named populations, and four species groups appear only on the newer list. Of those 110 entries, 83 were absent from the earlier list, and just 27 of the original 71 carried over. Each list names 30 plants, but only three appear on both. So where is project funding directed?

The two lists contain 154 distinct entries: 27 retained, 83 added and 44 absent from the newer list.

3. Priority places received AUD 56 million of AUD 147 million

Saving Native Species Phase 1 has 161 entries in the seven project streams used here, totalling AUD 147.43 million in listed funding. Priority places make up the largest amount, at AUD 56.08 million. The project-count and flow maps use the 143 entries assigned to one state. The priority-species map and later funding comparison use the 76 single-state entries in that stream.

4. Funding shares don’t match listing shares

New South Wales has 24.6% of the state-based threatened-species listings counted here, but 14.5% of the listed funding in this analysis. Tasmania shows the reverse: 7.3% of listings and 15.3% of funding. Listings give a rough indication of conservation need, so comparing them with funding puts the dollar totals in context.

Here, a listing means a nationally threatened species recorded in a state or territory under the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (EPBC) Act. Species found in several states are counted in each one.

The mix of listed species also differs between states.

Funding shows where effort is directed. The next section examines the evidence of improvement among earlier priorities.

5. Only 24 of 71 earlier priorities improved

An independent Year Five assessment found improved trajectories for 24 of the 71 earlier priorities. Fifteen of those improved entries were absent from the newer list, while seven entries with deteriorated trajectories stayed. That mix of outcomes raises another question: what can we learn by following one population for longer?

A longer record brings the outcome question down to one population.

The northern hairy-nosed wombat provides a separate view of change over decades. Its recovery plan plots selected population estimates from about 35 in 1982 to about 315 in 2021. Read alongside the mixed Year Five results, this series shows why repeated measurements matter when judging progress over time.

The ending depends on what we keep counting

By 2022, the score for monitored threatened populations stood at 42% of its 2000 level. Australia has redrawn its priority list and listed funding for new projects. The earlier Year Five assessment found that 24 of 71 priorities had improving trajectories. Those results cannot tell us whether the newer plan is working. The northern hairy-nosed wombat offers a longer view. Its estimated population rose from about 35 in 1982 to about 315 in 2021, despite losing roughly a tenth of its population to dingoes from 2000 to 2001. Continued monitoring will help show whether populations on the current priority list improve.
