Australia's Conservation Choices

🌿 A closer look at the decisions shaping Australia's wildlife future.

Introduction

Australia's threatened wildlife is under pressure. By 2022, an index of monitored threatened populations had declined to about 42% of its 2000 level.

The Australian Government introduced new conservation priorities and listed funding for projects.

But is conservation effort reaching the species and places that need it most?

Follow the evidence from population decline to changing priorities, funding decisions and conservation outcomes.

Australia's monitored threatened populations have fallen by more than half.

How did the decline unfold?

By 2022, the Threatened Species Index had fallen to about 42% of its 2000 level, after reaching a low of around 38% in 2019.

That figure describes relative change in monitored populations, not the proportion of species remaining. Although the index rose slightly after 2019, that rise does not yet establish sustained recovery.

Which species groups have suffered most?

Declines were uneven. The plotted reptile index fell furthest, while mammals fared comparatively better.

Amphibians and plants also declined sharply, though their reported ranges overlap with the reptiles' range, making their exact ranking less certain.

The national average hides different paths between groups

Australia's conservation priorities have changed significantly.

How were conservation priorities chosen?

With limited resources, governments must choose where to focus conservation efforts. The 2015–2020 strategy named 71 priorities and the 2022–2032 plan names 110, selected by independent scientists using six principles, including extinction risk and benefits to other species.

But this was more than an expansion. Most entries are new, and only three of the 30 priority plants appear on both lists. A change in priority status does not necessarily mean recovery or decline, since selection criteria and scientific assessments can change.

Only 27 priorities remained on both lists

Where is conservation funding going?

Most listed funding targets places and threats

The seven Saving Native Species Phase 1 streams analysed here list AUD 147.43 million across 161 project entries. Recovery action coordination is outside this comparison.

Priority places account for about 38% of the listed funding. Three threat streams, yellow crazy ants, gamba grass and threat innovation, add about 32%. Together they account for roughly seven in ten listed dollars. Emergency actions are shown separately. These are allocations, not confirmed spending.

Conservation funding supports practical work such as containing yellow crazy ants around Cairns and Townsville, controlling gamba grass in Kakadu, and addressing monitoring gaps for species including the northern hopping-mouse.

Funding priorities also differ by state. Queensland directs a larger share towards threat response, while Tasmania places greater emphasis on priority places.

Where are conservation projects concentrated?

The maps cover 143 of the 161 project entries with a single-state location. The shaded map focuses on the 76 targeting named priority species. Read together, shading shows priority-species funding, circles show project counts, and lines show total listed allocations.

Queensland's lead is far larger in total funding than in priority-species funding. Five states have roughly AUD 4–5 million in listed priority-species allocations, while South Australia has about AUD 1.47 million across four projects.

Queensland has the most single-state projects. But having more projects does not necessarily mean receiving more funding. Projects differ in size and cost.

Two yellow crazy ant projects account for AUD 24.8 million, about 93% of Queensland's mapped threat-response funding.

A few large projects can strongly influence a state's total listed allocation. This helps explain Queensland's much larger lead on the total-funding map.

Are states with more threatened species receiving more funding?

As a rough measure of conservation need, we compare each state's share of threatened-species listings with its share of listed funding for named priority species. New South Wales has a larger share of listings than funding, while Tasmania shows the opposite pattern.

In these 76 projects, funding does not follow listing shares directly. That does not mean any state is underfunded, since listing counts cannot capture extinction risk or conservation costs.

Why does the type of threatened species matter?

Species counts do not show which kinds of wildlife are at risk.

Plants account for the majority of listings in four states, while the heatmap reveals how counts differ across species groups.

Different species groups face different conservation needs.

Have conservation priorities shown signs of recovery?

Only 24 of 71 earlier priorities had improved trajectories

The Year Five report assessed outcomes for priorities in the 2015–2020 strategy.

Of 71 priorities, 24 had improved population trajectories, 32 showed no significant change and 15 deteriorated. An improved trajectory can still mean a population is declining, but more slowly.

Of the 24 improved priorities, 15 were not included on the newer list, while nine remained. However, changes in list status do not explain why populations rose or fell.

A recovery story: the northern hairy-nosed wombat

In 1982, an estimated 35 northern hairy-nosed wombats remained. By 2021, there were about 315 across two sites, despite dingoes killing almost a tenth of the population in 2000–01.

The species joined the priority list in 2022, and Phase 1 lists AUD 384,208 for a Queensland project that began in 2024. Because the estimates end before that project began, the earlier gains cannot be credited to its funding.

The long record shows how much can change over decades, and why continued monitoring matters when judging recovery.

The ending depends on what we keep counting

The challenge is not only choosing which species to protect, but directing money to meaningful interventions and measuring whether populations respond.

The earlier strategy had mixed outcomes, and the 2022–2032 plan is scheduled for assessment in 2027.

Success cannot be judged by funding or project counts alone, it requires lasting gains in threatened populations.

How to read the numbers and comparisons
  • Population index: The Threatened Species Index measures relative change in monitored threatened and near-threatened populations, with 2000 set to 1. It is not a count of remaining species; a subsequent increase alone does not establish sustained recovery.
  • Priority lists: 71 priorities in the 2015–2020 strategy and 110 species and populations in the 2022–2032 plan, compared across lists.
  • Funding: Phase 1 project tables contain 161 entries across seven streams. Amounts are listed allocations rather than confirmed spending. Four recovery-coordination projects named separately on the Phase 1 page are not included in these tables; GST treatment is not specified.
  • State maps: The maps include 143 single-state projects. Eighteen other entries are excluded because they are national, multi-state, in external territories or have conflicting location information. One conflicting entry is worth AUD 85,117.
  • Threatened-species listings: These are nationally listed Critically Endangered, Endangered and Vulnerable species recorded in each state or territory under the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (EPBC) Act (state lists, 28 August 2026). A species found in multiple states is counted in each relevant state.
  • Year Five outcomes: The Year Five report evaluates the 2015–2020 strategy. It does not evaluate the 2022–2032 plan, and changes in list status cannot explain why populations improved or deteriorated.
  • Funding–need comparison: State shares compare threatened-species listings with listed allocations from 76 single-state priority-species projects, not all conservation funding. Listing counts do not capture extinction risk, intervention costs or the benefits of projects supporting multiple species; differences cannot establish whether a state is underfunded.
  • Comparing funding over time: Earlier and newer programme funding is not compared directly because the available records use different reporting structures.
