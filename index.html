Australia's Conservation Choices

A national score for monitored threatened populations stood at 42% of its 2000 level by 2022. Australia's priority lists have changed and millions of dollars have been listed for projects. Where is that effort focused, and what evidence of recovery can we see?

1. Monitored populations fell to 42% of their 2000 level

Australia’s national score for monitored threatened populations starts at 1 in 2000. It reached its low of 0.383 in 2019 and stood at 0.417 in 2022. Reptiles had the lowest 2022 group score, while mammals declined less steeply. The figures make the scale of the challenge clear before we turn to the choices made in response.

The shared decline hides sharply different paths between groups.

2. Three in four newer priorities were absent from the earlier list

The 2022–2032 list contains 110 entries, including species and some named populations. Four groups entered the newer priority list. Of its entries, 83 were absent from the 2015–2020 priority list and only 27 carried over. Plants numbered 30 on each list, yet only three appeared on both. The lists show where national attention shifted; funding shows how some of that attention was put into projects.

The two lists contain 154 distinct entries: 27 retained, 83 added and 44 absent from the newer list.

3. Priority places received AUD 56 million of AUD 147 million

Saving Native Species Phase 1 has 161 entries in the seven project streams used here, totalling AUD 147.43 million in listed funding. Priority places make up the largest amount, at AUD 56.08 million. The state views focus on projects assigned to one state, revealing how the total changes by purpose and location.

The next three maps answer different questions: how much, how many entries, and where the listed allocations connect.

4. State listings and mapped funding have different patterns

The Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (EPBC) Act lists threatened species in each state. Plants account for about 71% of WA’s state entries and 65% of NSW’s. Comparing the share of these listings with mapped priority-species funding reveals the largest gap for NSW. That comparison describes the pattern of two measures; the outcome evidence comes next.

5. Only 24 of 71 earlier priorities improved

An independent Year Five assessment found improved trajectories for 24 of the 71 earlier priorities. Fifteen of those improved entries were absent from the newer list, while seven entries with deteriorated trajectories stayed. That mix of outcomes raises another question: what can we learn by following one population for longer?

A longer record brings the outcome question down to one population.

The northern hairy-nosed wombat provides a separate view of change over decades. Its recovery plan plots selected population estimates from about 35 in 1982 to about 315 in 2021. Read alongside the mixed Year Five results, this series shows why repeated measurements matter when judging progress over time.

Conclusion

Monitored populations declined, priority lists changed and listed funding varied by stream and state. Earlier outcomes were mixed, while the wombat estimates show that local recovery is possible. These sources cover different species and periods, so they leave the results of the 2022–2032 plan open for future measurement.
