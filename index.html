Australia's Conservation Choices

By 2022, an index of Australia’s monitored threatened populations had fallen to about 42% of its 2000 level. In 2022, the Australian Government named new conservation priorities and recorded funding for conservation projects. Where is that effort directed, and what evidence do we have of recovery? This page follows the changing priority lists, recorded project funding and the outcomes reported for the earlier priorities. It then uses the northern hairy-nosed wombat as a case study of what decades of population estimates can reveal. The newer priority species are scheduled for assessment in 2027.

1. Monitored populations declined, with reptiles falling furthest

After reaching an estimated low in 2019, the national score rose slightly by 2022 but remained far below its 2000 level. That small rise alone does not establish recovery. The group charts below reveal another part of the story.

The national figure hides how differently each group fared. By 2022, the reptile index had fallen to about 12% of its 2000 level, while the mammal index stood at about 65%. The panels below show the years behind those endpoints and how the gap developed over time.

Against this backdrop, which species and populations became national priorities?

2. Three in four newer priorities were absent from the earlier list

The 2015–2020 strategy named 71 priorities, while the 2022–2032 plan names 110 species and populations. Of the 110 newer entries, 83 were absent from the earlier list and 27 appear on both. Four species groups appear only on the newer list, but the shift also reaches groups on both lists. Each list names 30 plants, yet just three appear in both.

The two lists contain 154 distinct entries: 27 retained, 83 added and 44 absent from the newer list.

The lists show how national priorities changed. Where did project funding go?

3. Priority places lead funding, while state shares vary

Saving Native Species Phase 1 funds different kinds of conservation work. Priority places projects work across large areas to reduce threats to threatened species. Among the 161 project entries in the seven streams examined here, this stream has the largest amount of listed funding at AUD 56.08 million out of AUD 147.43 million. The maps use smaller sets: 143 entries assigned to one state for project counts and total allocations, and 76 single-state priority-species entries for the funding map.

Maps show three different measures of investment

Priority-species funding, project counts and total allocations answer different questions about where projects are recorded.

Funding shares differ from threatened-species listing shares

Among the 76 single-state priority-species projects, New South Wales has 14.5% of funding but 24.6% of the threatened-species listings counted across states and territories. Tasmania has 15.3% of that funding and 7.3% of listings. The comparison puts the project totals beside one rough measure of need.

Here, a listing means a nationally threatened species recorded in a state or territory under the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (EPBC) Act. Species found in several states are counted in each one.

Plants account for much of some states’ listings

The number of listings is only part of the picture. The mix of species also differs between states.

The projects show where current effort is recorded. What did the earlier strategy’s assessment find?

4. 24 of 71 earlier priorities improved

The Year Five assessment of the 2015–2020 strategy found that 24 of its 71 priorities improved, 32 showed no significant change and 15 deteriorated. Nine of the improved entries remained on the newer list, as did seven of the deteriorated entries. The lists do not explain why any entry stayed or left, and these earlier outcomes do not assess the newer plan.

What can a longer record reveal about recovery and setbacks?

The northern hairy-nosed wombat is one case study, not a measure of the newer plan’s results. Its recovery plan records estimates rising from about 35 animals in 1982 to about 315 in 2021, with a setback of roughly 10% after dingo attacks in 2000–01. That longer record shows why a single assessment cannot tell the whole story.

The ending depends on what we keep counting

We can see that monitored populations declined, national priorities changed and funding was recorded for new projects. We cannot yet judge the newer plan by the earlier strategy’s mixed results. The wombat’s longer record includes both a setback and substantial growth, showing the value of following populations over time. The planned 2027 assessment will offer the next opportunity to ask whether the newer priorities are improving.
